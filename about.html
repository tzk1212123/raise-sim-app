1 · The experiment: tuning how a liquid wets a surface

Place a small drop on a flat surface and look from the side. The angle between the drop's edge and the surface is the static contact angle. A large angle means a round bead (poor wetting); a small angle means the drop spreads (good wetting). Wettability matters for coatings, sprays, medical devices and textiles.

We dissolve two surfactants (soap-like molecules) in water:

  • SDS 94%: cheap, commercial-grade sodium dodecyl sulfate. On its own it spreads the drop strongly (≈ 61°).
  • Tween 20: a mild non-ionic surfactant. On its own the drop stays rounder (≈ 74–75°).

Goal: find the mixture whose contact angle is 72° (the performance of expensive 99%-pure SDS), while using as little total surfactant as possible. Each ingredient can be set from 0.04 to 0.60 / 1.20 % w/v in 0.04 steps: 450 possible recipes.

Task, numbers and protocol follow the RAISE self-driving lab (Nazeri et al., Digital Discovery 2026, arXiv 2510.06546), which ran this campaign on an Opentrons OT-2 robot.

2 · One test, step by step

  1. Calculate volumes from the recipe and the stock concentrations (200 µL total).
  2. Dispense water, SDS stock and Tween stock into a fresh well, each with a fresh tip (P20 for ≤ 20 µL, P300 above).
  3. Mix by pipetting 100 µL up and down 10 times.
  4. Place 3 droplets of 3 µL on unused spots of a stepped imaging stage.
  5. Pick up the camera with the pipette mount, move beside each droplet, wait 10 s.
  6. Capture a side image and fit the droplet contour; on a failed detection, wait 5 s and retry once.
  7. Report the mean and spread of the three angles. ≈ 3–3.5 min per recipe; 90 spots = 30 recipes per batch, then a person resets the deck.

3 · How the simulator imitates the experiment

The simulator is a software copy of the robot and the chemistry. Planners talk to it exactly as they would talk to the real robot: they send a recipe and get back three measured angles. Everything else is hidden from them.

In the real labIn the simulatorWhere the numbers come from
The liquid's true contact angle for each recipeHidden "true" surface over all 450 recipes Rebuilt from the RAISE paper's published figures (raw data is not public); a physics-based model (mixed micelles → surface adsorption → wetting) fitted to them, cross-checked by a second, data-only model
OT-2 robot, pipettes, tips, plateDevices with the same actions (pick tip, transfer, mix, place droplet…) Opentrons P20 / P300 volume limits are enforced; pipetting error grows at small volumes; tips, wells and stocks are counted
Imaging stage and camera2 stages × 5 levels × 9 spots; camera pick-up, positioning, capture RAISE deck layout and protocol (10 s wait, one retry)
Measurement imperfectionMeasurement model Per-level offsets and ~0.55° scatter from RAISE water tests (Table S2); +~0.6° for surfactant solutions; 2% gross outliers; 3% failed detections
Time and peopleSimulated clock; manual reset every 30 recipes Per-step timings tuned to RAISE throughput (≈ 3 min per recipe); human reset time logged separately

What a planner sees vs. what stays hidden

planner sees
  • task description and limits
  • its own results: 3 droplet angles, mean ± SD, score
  • failures as missing values
hidden (used only for grading)
  • true angle of every recipe
  • which readings were outliers
  • the best possible recipe

Because the simulator knows the truth, we can grade each planner's final answer exactly, something impossible in a real lab.

The hidden true surface

Colour = true contact angle. White outline = good recipes (on target and thrifty). ★ = best recipe.

Limits: stock concentrations, mixture volume and timings are assumptions until measured in our lab; the model under-predicts angles in the low-SDS corner by up to ~3°. The simulator compares strategies fairly; it does not predict exact lab numbers.

4 · How contestants are compared

Same labEvery contestant gets its own copy of the same simulated world, the same random starter tests and the same noise.
Same budgetAt most N tests each. Fewer is better: each test costs robot time and consumables.
One answerEach contestant names one recipe. We check its true angle (on target = 70.4–73.6°) and re-test it 5× on a fresh setup.
Score100 × √[(1 − |angle − 72|/50) × (1 − total/2)] — the RAISE desirability. 0 if the answer misses the target.
Many repeatsOne comparison is anecdotal; the repeated-study page runs dozens and reports averages with 95% intervals.

Contestants: Random, OFAT (one factor at a time, like a person), two Bayesian optimisers (the RAISE method and an improved variant), and LLM scientists (DeepSeek, GPT, Gemini, Qwen, Kimi, GLM, Claude…) that read results, reason, and choose the next test.
